Two hundred applications.
Two replies.

If that is your inbox, nothing is wrong with you. Your experience is arriving untranslated, and translation is a fixable problem. Here is where it breaks, in order.

1
Aim

You are applying to everything, and it is making every application worse.

I apply to everything I can find. Forty this month. Nothing sticks. Where most people start

Applying widely feels like effort. It is usually avoidance of a harder decision: choosing. And it quietly costs you twice, because forty generic applications are worse than eight specific ones, not better.

From my side of the table, a generic application is obvious in about ten seconds. It says nothing about us. If you cannot say why this company, I cannot argue for you in a hiring meeting, even if I want to.

The move

Stop asking what jobs exist. Ask what fits: your real expertise, your real interests, and where the market actually is. The output is a short list you can hold in your head, with a written reason beside every name.

What changes

Eight companies you can talk about instead of forty you cannot. Every application after this gets better, because they all start from the same clear answer to why you and why them.

2
Get Found

They looked you up. There was nothing there.

Nobody knows I exist. I am invisible here. The quietest part of the problem

Here is what actually happens when your application does well. Somebody types your name into a search box. What they find decides whether you are a person or a PDF.

Most people hear this and think self promotion, which they hate, so they do nothing. That is the wrong read. This is not about pushing yourself at people. It is about becoming the person they associate with one thing, so they come to you when that thing comes up.

The move

Pick one lane and be visible in it. Show the work you are already doing, comment properly where that conversation is happening, and stay consistent long enough that your name and that topic get linked in people's heads.

What changes

People start associating you with the thing. The direction reverses: instead of you chasing roles, some of them arrive because someone already thinks of you when that problem comes up.

3
Get Answered

Your experience is not worth less here. It is just untranslated.

I get no reply. Not even a no. Just silence. The most common message I get

In most of the world it is normal, and polite, to describe your job by its responsibilities and to give the team credit. Here, that same modesty reads as vague. And vague loses, because the reader cannot tell what you actually did.

This is not about claiming more than you did. It is about naming exactly what was yours.

Before

Responsible for management of digital banking products for 8 years

After

Owned a banking app used by 2M people. Shipped 14 releases a year with a team of six.

The move

Translate every line from duty into outcome. Say what you decided, not what you were assigned. Carry your own scale, because a recruiter here may not know your last employer is huge at home. Keep your title and add the scope.

What changes

The same eight years, read properly. Replies instead of silence, from the same record you already had.

4
Connect

Most people only reach out when they need something. That is exactly why it does not work.

I do not know anyone here. I have no network. Said by almost everyone who moves

A referred candidate does not wait in the same queue. That is not unfair, it is just how risk works: someone is vouching, so the cost of interviewing you drops.

The reason networking fails for most people is not shyness. It is that they only appear when they need something. Nobody wants to be the person you contact only when you want a job, and everyone can feel it.

The move

Remember people properly. What they care about, what they are working on, what you last talked about. Then be useful before you need anything, on purpose, on a schedule. No tricks, no templates, no automated sending. Thoughtful at scale, never mechanical.

What changes

In six months you have twenty people who know what you are working on, because you have been genuinely interested in what they are working on. A wide circle that remembers you beats a small circle you exhaust. When you finally need something, you are not a stranger.

5
Interviews

The interviewer is your user. Each round wants a different thing.

I get to the interview and then it just ends. The last mile, where good people lose

There is no such thing as "the interview". There are four or five different rooms, and each one is judging something different. Most people tell the same story the same way in all of them, and lose rounds they should have won.

The HR screen wants to know you are real and roughly affordable. The hiring manager wants to know you can solve the problem they live with. The team wants to know if they want to sit beside you. The panel wants to see how you think under pressure. Leadership wants to know if you see past your own task.

The move

Read the room before the room. Find out who you are meeting and what they are judged on, then choose which of your stories is actually for them. And when the salary question comes early, do not name your number first.

What changes

You walk into each round knowing what that person needs to hear, with a story chosen for them and a question ready to ask back. And when the offer comes, you know the first number is rarely the last.
